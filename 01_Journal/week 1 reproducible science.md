In the FB example they fed an intentional feed to users, “something seemingly they do with or without intention anyway”. On one hand positive and the other negative, “like they knew what was positive or not from the perception of the user” to measure the reactions by means of emojis and chat. My real issue with this is the idea that the feeds are not already catered or even a good mix of positive or negative, and that the person felt the vid or content was negative since they might like what FB thought was negative in the first place. How would Fb even know unless the feeds were tailored to each person based on their beliefs. Kramer et al. (2014) Next with this data set would be the crowd reaction, having started with the subjects bias to negative or positive content if you did manage to give a person all negative feeds then wonder why their feedback was negative seems kinda basic in any psychological sense, and lacks abductive reasoning almost like they didn’t ask other disciplines if people react negatively to negativity, shocker, we do. So that would be a problem in the question itself making the whole experiment questionable in gaining new knowledge. It did make me wonder why they didn’t implement the question of why do people consume sad or negative media, like rage bait, seems there is a lot to learn in that sphere. As the readings suggested there is a lot of noise that may have made this study muddy. Some research argued that the sample size was too small,  but 700000 people polled seems like more than enough to be able to gain a reasonable conclusion, if most of the sample reacted as expected or opposed to the initial question consistently. In the ethics dilemma people that engage on social media have more choice than is assumed here. In my own observation I can manipulate a feed as can a lot of people and has been shown in mass media. Meaning this social experiment isn’t limited to the way meta has structured their data set but the reverse can also be done to the algorithm. Bandura (1978). When you reverse it,does it pass ethical considerations like, “Is it equal on all sides”. Since users can change the channel or manipulate the system itself it would seem that facebook may not have had good intentions but failed to see how they were being done the same way. This study looks more like an example of not gaining knowledge of a good question with a good literature review, but more of a “We could do this” and just went for it with reckless abandon.

    this is not a hypothetical. This is already established in psychological and communication research. Decades of studies on emotional response to stimuli show that people consistently react negatively to negative content. The Facebook researchers either ignored this literature or failed to consult it. Cross-disciplinary consultation would have reshaped what they were trying to find in the first place.

Ai’s take.

    The Facebook study raises serious ethical and methodological questions. First, the classification of posts as 'positive' or 'negative' relied on a crude word-count algorithm (LIWC) that cannot account for context or individual interpretation. What Facebook labeled 'negative'—a post about a political opponent—might be interpreted as 'positive' by a supporter. Second, the study assumed that reducing the volume of positive or negative posts would produce uniform emotional effects, ignoring individual differences in emotional response. This is a basic failure in psychological research: people react to content based on their pre-existing beliefs, not the content in isolation. Third, the study's effect size was tiny (0.04%), raising questions about whether the findings were practically meaningful. Finally, the ethical failure—conducting research on nearly 700,000 people without informed consent—undermines whatever scientific value the study might have had. The study produced some knowledge, but it was not worth the cost to public trust or ethical standards.
